## Why radio skills matter before your first lesson

Aviation radio communication feels intimidating at first. The pace is fast, the vocabulary is unfamiliar, and everyone on the frequency sounds like they have been doing it forever. The good news: the patterns are rigid and predictable. Once you internalize the four-part structure and can say the phonetic alphabet without thinking, 80 percent of the anxiety disappears.

You do not need to be perfect before your first flight lesson — your instructor will handle the radios if necessary. But knowing the basics means you can listen to what is happening, understand ATC instructions, and start building the muscle memory early. Radio proficiency compounds: every hour you spend listening to LiveATC before you ever sit in a cockpit is worth it.

## The phonetic alphabet

ICAO (the International Civil Aviation Organization) standardized a phonetic alphabet so that letters cannot be misheard over a noisy radio. "N" and "M" sound alike; "November" and "Mike" do not.

You will use it constantly: spelling your callsign, taxiway names, waypoints, and more. The goal is to say the words reflexively — when ATC says "taxi via Alpha Bravo," you should know immediately that means taxiways A and B.

## How aviation numbers are spoken

Aviation uses a modified pronunciation for digits to prevent mishearing over noisy radios. The critical ones:

| Digit | Aviation pronunciation |
|-------|------------------------|
| 0 | ZERO |
| 1 | WUN |
| 2 | TOO |
| 3 | TREE |
| 4 | FOWER |
| 5 | FIFE |
| 6 | SIX |
| 7 | SEVEN |
| 8 | AIT |
| 9 | NINER |

The word "niner" (not "nine") avoids confusion with the German word "nein," which means "no." During early transatlantic aviation, this distinction mattered enormously.

**How numbers are grouped depends on context:**

- **Frequencies** — Each digit separately, with "POINT" for the decimal. 121.6 MHz = "ONE TWO ONE POINT SIX."
- **Altitudes** — Thousands and hundreds. 5,500 ft = "FIVE THOUSAND FIVE HUNDRED."
- **Headings** — Each digit separately. Runway 18 heading = "ONE EIGHT ZERO."
- **Transponder codes** — Each digit separately. Code 1200 = "ONE TWO ZERO ZERO."
- **Runway numbers** — Each digit separately. Runway 27 = "TWO SEVEN."

## The four-part call structure

Every initial radio call follows the same four-part pattern:

1. **Who you are calling** — the ATC facility
2. **Who you are** — your callsign
3. **Where you are** — your position
4. **What you want** — your request

This order is not arbitrary. ATC controllers are managing multiple frequencies and aircraft. Hearing the facility name first confirms the call is for them. Hearing your callsign second lets them pull up your information. Hearing your position and request last means they have context before they respond.

A complete ground call looks like this:

> "Runway City Ground, Cessna One Two Three Foxtrot Sierra, FBO ramp, request taxi to Runway One Eight, VFR departure to the north."

## What to read back

A **readback** is your confirmation that you copied an ATC instruction correctly. It is not optional — FAR 91.123 requires you to comply with ATC instructions, and reading back clearances is how ATC confirms you understood correctly.

**Always read back:**
- Runway assignments ("cleared for takeoff Runway One Eight")
- Hold-short instructions ("hold short of Runway Zero Nine Two Seven")
- Squawk codes ("squawk four five two one")
- Altimeter settings ("altimeter two niner niner two")
- Frequency changes ("contact Approach on one two four point four")
- Altitude assignments on IFR clearances

**You do NOT need to read back:**
- Taxi routes (unless you are unsure)
- General advisories ("traffic in the area, please advise")
- ATIS acknowledgement callsigns alone

A correct readback for a taxi clearance sounds like:

> "Taxi via Alpha, hold short of Runway Zero Nine Two Seven, Cessna One Two Three Foxtrot Sierra."

Note that your callsign comes at the **end** of a readback — the reverse of an initial call. This convention lets the controller know who is reading back.

If you miss something in a clearance, say **"say again"** — never "what?" or "huh?" Partial readbacks are acceptable; always read back what you heard and ask the controller to repeat any portion you missed.

## Class D airspace and two-way communication

Most training airports have an operating control tower, which means they are surrounded by **Class D airspace**. The key rule for Class D:

> You must establish **two-way radio communication** with the tower **before entering** the Class D airspace. (AIM 3-2-5)

"Establishing two-way communication" means ATC has acknowledged your callsign by name. If the controller says "aircraft calling, standby," that is **not** two-way communication — they did not use your callsign. You must wait until they say your callsign before entering.

Class D typically extends from the surface to 2,500 ft AGL and has a radius that varies by airport. The boundaries are shown as a dashed blue circle on sectional charts.

When the control tower is closed, Class D ceases to exist. The airspace reverts to Class E or G (check the Chart Supplement for specifics). No radio contact is then required, though self-announcing on CTAF is strongly recommended.

**ATIS — Automatic Terminal Information Service:** Before calling ground or clearance delivery, listen to ATIS (a recorded broadcast of current weather, active runways, and NOTAMs). When you call, acknowledge it with "information [phonetic letter]" — for example, "information Bravo." This tells the controller you have the current weather and they do not need to repeat it.
