## Why surface operations matter

Runway incursions — an aircraft, vehicle, or person entering a runway without authorization — are the leading cause of fatal accidents at towered airports. The FAA's Runway Safety Program exists specifically to reduce these events, and the numbers still run into the hundreds each year. Most incursions are not caused by reckless pilots; they are caused by distraction, misunderstood clearances, and complacency on "routine" taxi operations.

The FAA maintains a list of **runway safety hotspots** at complex airports — locations historically prone to confusion, poor sight lines, or clearance ambiguity. These hotspots appear on FAA airport diagrams (and in ForeFlight and Garmin Pilot) as numbered circles. Knowing they exist is the first step; understanding *why* a given intersection is dangerous is what changes behavior.

Surface operations demand the same discipline as airborne operations. Brief every taxi clearance before you release the brakes.

## Know before you go

Before calling ground, have three things ready:

1. **ATIS** — Active runways, NOTAMs, weather, and altimeter. The ATIS also tells you which taxiways may be closed. Write down the information code.
2. **Airport diagram** — Available in ForeFlight, Garmin Pilot, and the FAA Chart Supplement. Study it on the ground, not while moving. Know where you are parked, where the runway is, and what taxiways connect them.
3. **A pencil** — Write down your taxi clearance as you receive it. At complex airports, clearances can be long. Reading it back from your notes is faster and more accurate than reading it back from memory.

The goal is to build a **mental map** before you move. Taxi operations at an unfamiliar airport should feel like a planned route, not an improvised drive.

## Standard taxi clearance anatomy

Every taxi clearance from Ground follows a predictable structure:

> **[Callsign], [Facility], taxi to [Runway] via [Taxiways], [any crossing or hold-short instructions].**

Example:
> *"November One Two Three Foxtrot Sierra, Runway City Ground, taxi to Runway One Eight via Alpha, hold short of Runway Zero-Niner Two-Seven."*

Break it into parts as you hear it:

| Element | Example | What to do |
|---|---|---|
| Callsign | N123FS | Confirm it's you |
| Destination runway | Runway 18 | Write it down |
| Route | via Alpha | Trace it on your diagram |
| Crossing / hold-short | hold short of 09/27 | **Read back explicitly** |

## The critical rule: runway entry requires explicit authorization

This rule is unambiguous: **you may not enter or cross a runway without an explicit ATC clearance to do so.**

Hold-short bars (four yellow lines — two solid, two dashed — painted across a taxiway) are **absolute stop points**. Coming to a complete stop before those bars is not optional. Even if you can see clearly that the runway is empty, even if you are behind schedule, even if another aircraft is waiting behind you — you stop.

**Read back ALL runway assignments and hold-short instructions.** This is an FAA regulatory requirement (AIM 4-3-18). The readback is not a formality; it is a cross-check that confirms ATC and pilot have the same understanding. If your readback contains an error, ATC will correct you before you move.

When in doubt, **stop and ask**. Calling ground to confirm your position or ask for progressive taxi instructions is never the wrong move. Controllers prefer the call.

## Hotspots

A **runway safety hotspot** is a location on an airport surface where the risk of runway incursion or surface incident is elevated. The FAA designates hotspots based on accident history, operational complexity, or geometry that is prone to confusion.

Hotspots appear on FAA airport diagrams as pink or red circles labeled HS1, HS2, etc. Each hotspot has a written description in the Chart Supplement (formerly the Airport/Facility Directory) explaining the specific risk. ForeFlight and Garmin Pilot display hotspot overlays in their moving map views.

Before taxiing at an unfamiliar towered airport, check the airport diagram for hotspot locations and read the descriptions. At the fictional Runway City Airport used in this lesson, **HS1** marks the Taxiway A crossing of Runway 09/27 — a crossing that requires an explicit clearance and where the runway is not visible from the hold-short line until you are very close.
