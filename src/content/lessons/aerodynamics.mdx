## Four forces of flight

Every aircraft in flight is acted upon by four forces:

- **Lift** — the upward aerodynamic force produced by the wings, acting perpendicular to the relative wind.
- **Weight** — the downward pull of gravity acting through the aircraft's center of gravity.
- **Thrust** — the forward force produced by the engine and propeller (or jet engine), overcoming drag.
- **Drag** — the rearward aerodynamic resistance opposing motion through the air.

In straight-and-level, unaccelerated flight, lift equals weight and thrust equals drag. Any imbalance causes the aircraft to accelerate in the direction of the unbalanced force.

## Angle of attack

**Angle of attack (AoA)** is the angle between the wing's chord line and the **relative wind** — the direction the air appears to be coming from, as seen from the aircraft. It is not the same as pitch attitude.

A pilot can be pitched nose-high with a low AoA (climbing fast, so the relative wind still comes from ahead), or pitched nose-low with a high AoA (pulling hard out of a dive). AoA and pitch attitude are related but independent.

As AoA increases, lift increases — up to the **critical AoA** (roughly 17–18° for a typical wing). Beyond the critical AoA, airflow separates from the upper wing surface and lift collapses. This is a **stall**.

## The lift equation

Lift is described by:

**L = ½ ρ V² C&#8319; S**

Where:
- **ρ** (rho) — air density (decreases with altitude)
- **V** — true airspeed (lift grows with the *square* of speed)
- **C&#8319;** — lift coefficient (set by AoA and wing shape)
- **S** — wing area

The key insight: you can produce the same lift by using **more speed** (higher V) *or* **more AoA** (higher C&#8319;), or any combination. At slow speeds the wing must fly at a higher AoA to maintain lift; at high speeds it can fly at a very low AoA.

## Stalls

A stall occurs when the **critical AoA is exceeded** — not when airspeed drops below some fixed number. Consequences:

- You can stall at any airspeed, in any attitude.
- You can stall in a steep-banked turn at cruise speed by pulling back aggressively.
- You can stall climbing or descending, upright or inverted.

Recovery always requires reducing AoA (pushing forward on the controls), regardless of attitude or altitude. Adding power helps recover altitude but does not by itself unstall the wing.

## Load factor and turns

In a **coordinated level turn**, the wings must produce enough lift to both support the aircraft's weight *and* provide the centripetal force that curves the flight path. The result is a greater force on the structure — the **load factor**, measured in g.

The relationship is:

**n = 1 / cos(bank angle)**

| Bank angle | Load factor |
|-----------|------------|
| 0° | 1.0 g |
| 30° | 1.15 g |
| 45° | 1.41 g |
| 60° | 2.0 g |
| 80° | 5.76 g |

A 60° bank doubles the structural load. The aircraft's stall speed also rises with load factor:

**Vs_turn = Vs_1g × √n**

At a 60° bank (n = 2), stall speed rises by a factor of √2 ≈ 1.41. A C172S with a 1-g stall speed of 48 kt will stall at about 68 kt in a 60° bank.
